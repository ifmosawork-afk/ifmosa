Why Cold Calling Works in Asia

Asia is a diverse continent with two distinct regions for job seekers: East Asia (Japan, South Korea, China) and Southeast Asia (Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam, Philippines). Hiring cultures, communication styles, and visa pathways vary significantly. What works in Japan may not work in Thailand. What's acceptable in Singapore might be too direct in Vietnam. Cold calling allows you to:

  • Bypass formal application processes that vary by country.
  • Demonstrate initiative and confidence – qualities that are valued across Asia, but expressed differently.
  • Learn about unadvertised roles in fast‑growing sectors like tech, finance, and manufacturing.

Visa context: Work visa systems vary widely across Asia:

  • Japan: Specified Skilled Worker (SSW) for 14 sectors, Engineer/Specialist in Humanities visa.
  • China: Z‑visa (work permit) – categories A, B, C based on qualifications.
  • Singapore: Employment Pass (EP) – salary threshold of S$5,000/month.
  • South Korea: E‑7 visa for skilled workers, E‑2 for teaching.
  • Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam, Philippines: Each has its own work permit system – research before calling.

🗾 East Asia – Japan, South Korea, China

🇯🇵 Japan 🇰🇷 South Korea 🇨🇳 China

Cultural Characteristics

East Asian business culture is characterised by hierarchy, formality, and relationship‑building. Cold calling without preparation is unlikely to succeed – you need a strategy.

  • Japan – risk‑averse and hierarchy‑protective. Cold calls without a specific name are blocked fast. Consensus‑building is critical.
  • South Korea – relationship‑driven; organisational fit and character are highly valued.
  • China – Guanxi (关系) – personal networks of mutual trust and obligation – are essential. An approach without guanxi has significantly lower probability of a meaningful response.

Key insight: In East Asia, business is built on trust and relationships. A cold call is not the end goal – it's the first step in a longer relationship‑building process.

Visa Context

  • Japan: Specified Skilled Worker (SSW) – 14 sectors including nursing, construction, manufacturing. Engineer/Specialist in Humanities visa for professionals.
  • South Korea: E‑7 visa for skilled workers, E‑2 for teaching English. Requires a job offer from a Korean employer.
  • China: Z‑visa (work permit) – categories A (highly skilled), B (professional), C (temporary). Requires a work permit from Chinese authorities.

Best Time to Call

  • Japan (JST): 10:00 – 11:30 AM or 2:00 – 4:00 PM. Avoid Monday mornings, month‑end, and busy cutoff dates. Call before 9 AM, just after lunch, or after 6 PM to reduce gatekeeper interference.
  • South Korea (KST): 10:00 – 11:30 AM or 2:00 – 4:00 PM. Avoid lunch (12‑1 PM).
  • China (CST): 10:00 – 11:30 AM or 2:00 – 4:00 PM. Avoid calling during national holidays (Golden Week, Chinese New Year).

Scripts – East Asia (formal, respectful, relationship‑building)

Scenario A: You found a job ad (Japan – formal and prepared)

"Dear Mr./Ms. [Hiring Manager's Full Name], this is [Your Full Name]. I apologise for the sudden contact. I saw your job posting for [Role] on [Platform] and I'm very interested. I have [X] years of experience in [field] and I'm particularly strong in [specific skill]. I apologise for taking your time, but could I have a few minutes to discuss the [Role]? I'd be happy to send you my CV."

Note: In Japan, use formal language and apologise for the interruption. Always have a specific name – calls without a name are blocked fast.

Scenario B: Exploring unadvertised opportunities (China – guanxi‑building)

"Hello Mr./Ms. [Hiring Manager's Name], this is [Your Full Name]. I've been following your company and I'm very impressed by your achievements in [industry]. I have a background in [field] and [X] years of experience in [skill]. I was wondering if your company has any upcoming hiring plans, or if there's anywhere I could contribute. Would you be available for a brief chat?"

Note: In China, guanxi (relationships) matter. If you have any mutual connection, mention it. A cold approach without guanxi has a low probability of success. Building a relationship is essential before asking for anything.

Scenario C: You need sponsorship (South Korea – professional and honest)

"Hello Mr./Ms. [Hiring Manager's Name], this is [Your Full Name]. I'm very interested in [Company] and I believe my experience in [field] could be valuable to your team. To be honest, I'm currently based outside Korea and would need visa sponsorship. I understand this is a challenge, but I wanted to ask if it's possible. Does your company consider sponsorship for the right candidate?"

Follow‑up email (formal, respectful)

Subject: Follow‑up – [Your Full Name] – [Role] interest

Dear Mr./Ms. [Hiring Manager's Last Name],

Thank you for taking the time to speak with me today. I greatly appreciated your insights into the [Role] position and the challenges your team is currently facing.

As I mentioned, I have extensive experience in [specific area] and I'm confident I could contribute meaningfully to your objectives. I have attached my CV and a brief portfolio of my work for your reference.

I would welcome the opportunity to continue the conversation at your convenience. Please let me know a time that suits you.

Yours sincerely,
[Your Full Name]

Note: In Japan and South Korea, formal titles and honorifics are essential. In China, follow‑ups should be polite and may be more indirect.

Common Objections

"We don't sponsor visas." (Japan)

Response: "I understand. Is that a fixed policy, or could it vary by role? I'm open to exploring other options if that's possible. Thank you for your time."

"We prefer local candidates." (China)

Response: "I completely understand. If my skills are a strong match, would you consider sponsorship? I'm confident I can add significant value to your team."

"We don't have a sponsor licence." (South Korea)

Response: "I understand. Is that something the company is considering? I appreciate your honesty."

East Asia‑Specific FAQ

Is cold calling common in East Asia?
It's less common than in Western countries. In Japan, cold calls without a name are blocked fast. In China, guanxi (relationships) are essential. In South Korea, referrals and networking are preferred over cold outreach.
Should I speak the local language?
English is used in multinational companies, but showing effort in the local language is highly appreciated. In Japan, even a few words of Japanese can open doors.
What is guanxi and why does it matter in China?
Guanxi (关系) is a Chinese concept describing a network of relationships built on mutual trust, reciprocal obligation, and sustained personal investment. In Chinese professional culture, an approach without guanxi has significantly lower probability of a meaningful response.

🌴 Southeast Asia – Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam, Philippines

🇸🇬 Singapore 🇲🇾 Malaysia 🇹🇭 Thailand 🇻🇳 Vietnam 🇵🇭 Philippines

Cultural Characteristics

Southeast Asia is a diverse region where relationships, respect, and indirect communication are valued. Cold calling can work, but it requires a different approach than in Western markets.

  • Singapore – most Westernised in the region. Cold calling is accepted in sales roles, but chasing HR for job status is seen as "too desperate". Email follow‑ups are preferred over phone.
  • Malaysia – relationship‑focused; building trust is important before discussing business.
  • Thailand – relationship‑oriented; a generic LinkedIn message or uninspiring cold call tells an experienced executive that little preparation has gone into the conversation.
  • Vietnam – respectful tone and permission‑seeking are essential. A 20/80 rule applies: out of 10 calls, only 2 people will answer – that's success.
  • Philippines – friendly and relationship‑driven; building rapport is important.

Key insight: Pickup rates in Southeast Asia range from 8‑12%, but calls alone won't get you far. Business is built on relationships.

Visa Context

  • Singapore: Employment Pass (EP) – salary threshold of S$5,000/month. S‑Pass for mid‑skilled workers.
  • Malaysia: Employment Pass – requires a job offer and salary threshold.
  • Thailand: Non‑Immigrant B visa – requires a job offer and work permit. BOI‑promoted companies streamline the process.
  • Vietnam: Work permit – requires a job offer and approval from Vietnamese authorities.
  • Philippines: Alien Employment Permit (AEP) – requires a job offer and approval from the Department of Labor.

Best Time to Call

  • Singapore (SGT): 10:00 – 11:30 AM or 2:00 – 4:00 PM. Tuesday – Thursday.
  • Malaysia (MYT): 10:00 – 11:30 AM or 2:00 – 4:00 PM.
  • Thailand (ICT): 10:00 – 11:30 AM or 2:00 – 4:00 PM. Avoid 12‑1 PM.
  • Vietnam (ICT): 9:00 – 11:00 AM or 2:00 – 4:00 PM.
  • Philippines (PHT): 10:00 – 11:30 AM or 2:00 – 4:00 PM.

Scripts – Southeast Asia (respectful, relationship‑building, prepared)

Scenario A: You found a job ad (Singapore – professional and direct)

"Good morning [Hiring Manager's Name], this is [Your Full Name]. I saw your job posting for the [Role] position on [Platform] and I was very interested. I have a background in [field] with [X] years of experience, and I've worked on projects similar to what you're describing. I don't want to take up too much of your time – I just wanted to introduce myself and ask if you'd be open to receiving my application directly. I can send my CV over right after this call."

Note: In Singapore, a direct but polite approach works well. However, chasing HR for status is seen as "too desperate" – follow up once via email, not multiple calls.

Scenario B: Exploring unadvertised roles (Thailand – warm and prepared)

"Good morning [Hiring Manager's Name], this is [Your Full Name]. I hope you're having a good day. I've been following [Company] with great interest – especially your recent work in [area]. I have experience in [skill] and I'm currently exploring opportunities where I can apply that experience. I understand you might not have an immediate opening, but I wanted to introduce myself and ask if you'd be open to a brief conversation about your team's priorities. I'd be happy to send my CV for your reference."

Note: In Thailand, a generic LinkedIn message or uninspiring cold call tells an experienced executive that little preparation has gone into the conversation. Be specific about why you're interested in their company.

Scenario C: You need sponsorship (Vietnam – respectful and honest)

"Hello [Hiring Manager's Name], this is [Your Full Name]. I'm calling because I'm very interested in [Company] and I believe my experience in [field] could be a good fit. I want to be transparent: I'm currently based outside Vietnam and I would need sponsorship. I understand this can be a challenge, and I don't want to waste your time if it's not possible. Could you tell me if your company has ever sponsored international hires in similar roles?"

Note: In Vietnam, a respectful tone and permission‑seeking are essential. The 20/80 rule applies – out of 10 calls, only 2 people will answer, and that's success. Don't be discouraged by unanswered calls.

Scenario D: Following up after an application (Philippines – friendly and professional)

"Good morning [Hiring Manager's Name], this is [Your Full Name]. I recently applied for the [Role] position through your careers portal and I wanted to follow up. I'm very interested in [Company] and I wanted to put a voice to my application. I have [X] years of experience in [field] and I'm confident I could contribute to your team. Would you have a few minutes to discuss my application?"

Follow‑up email (warm and professional)

Subject: Follow‑up – [Your Full Name] – [Role] interest

Dear [Hiring Manager's Name],

It was a pleasure speaking with you earlier today. Thank you for taking the time to share your perspective on the [Role] opportunity and the challenges your team is facing.

As I mentioned, I have extensive experience in [specific area] and I believe I could contribute to [Company]'s goals in a meaningful way. I've attached my CV for your reference.

I hope we can continue the conversation soon. Please don't hesitate to reach out if you have any questions.

Warm regards,
[Your Full Name]

Southeast Asia‑Specific FAQ

Is cold calling effective in Southeast Asia?
Pickup rates range from 8‑12%, but calls alone won't get you far. Business is built on relationships. A call is the first step – follow up with emails and LinkedIn messages to build rapport.
Should I speak English or the local language?
English is widely used in business, especially in Singapore, Malaysia, and the Philippines. In Thailand and Vietnam, showing effort in the local language is appreciated.
Is it acceptable to follow up on job applications in Singapore?
Chasing HR for status is seen as "too desperate". Email once a week is acceptable – avoid multiple phone calls.

The IFMOSA 5‑Step Method – Asia Edition

This is our proprietary framework, adapted for Asia's diverse markets. Follow these steps to maximise your success rate.

1. Identify Target Companies

Focus on companies with a history of hiring international talent. Priority sectors:

  • Japan: Tech, healthcare, manufacturing, construction (SSW sectors).
  • China: Tech, finance, education (ESL teaching), manufacturing.
  • Singapore: Tech, finance, fintech, logistics.
  • Thailand, Vietnam, Malaysia: Tech, manufacturing, tourism, hospitality.

2. Find the Decision‑Maker

Asian hiring managers are often Department Heads, HR Managers, or Recruitment Managers. LinkedIn is widely used in Singapore, India, and major Asian hubs. In Japan, use LinkedIn or sites like Wantedly. In China, WeChat and LinkedIn are common.

Title tips:

  • Japan: HR department or recruitment manager.
  • China: HR manager or recruitment manager.
  • South Korea: HR team or recruitment manager.
  • Singapore: Talent Acquisition Manager or HR Manager.

3. Get Their Direct Contact

Email patterns vary by country:

  • Japan: firstname.lastname@company.co.jp
  • South Korea: firstname.lastname@company.co.kr
  • China: Varies – check the company website.
  • Singapore: firstname.lastname@company.sg

In Japan, calling without a specific name is likely to be blocked. Always have a name before you dial.

4. Make the Call – Region‑Specific Scripts

The script you use should match the region's communication style. See the region‑specific guides above for tailored scripts.

General Asia script (start here – but adapt to local culture):
"Good morning/afternoon [Hiring Manager’s Name], this is [Your Name]. I'm calling because I'm very interested in [Company Name] and the work your team is doing in [area]. I have a background in [skill] and I'd love to learn about any upcoming opportunities. Do you have a few minutes?"

If you need work visa sponsorship, ask after establishing interest:

"Before I take more of your time – I'm currently based outside [country] and would need sponsorship. Is that something your company is able to support for the right candidate?"

Note: In East Asia, it's better to build a relationship before asking about sponsorship. In Southeast Asia, direct but respectful is acceptable.

5. Follow Up Professionally

Send a thank‑you email within 24 hours. In Japan and South Korea, follow‑ups should be formal and polite. In Singapore, email once a week is acceptable – avoid multiple phone calls.

Frequently Asked Questions – Cold Calling in Asia

Is cold calling effective across all of Asia?
Yes, but the approach must be tailored to the region. In East Asia (Japan, China, South Korea), relationships and formalities are essential. In Southeast Asia (Singapore, Thailand, Vietnam), respect and preparation matter.
Do I need to speak the local language?
English is widely accepted in business, especially in Singapore and multinational companies. However, showing effort in the local language is always appreciated – especially in Japan, China, and South Korea.
What is the best way to find a hiring manager's name in Asia?
LinkedIn is widely used across Asia. In Japan, try Wantedly. In China, WeChat and LinkedIn are common. If you can't find a name, call the main line and ask politely for the person responsible for hiring in your department.
Can I call a hiring manager without a job offer?
Yes – but in East Asia, you need to build a relationship first. In Singapore and Southeast Asia, speculative applications are more accepted.
How long should my call be?
Keep it under 5 minutes unless the manager engages you further. Be respectful of their time. In Japan, be prepared for a longer relationship‑building process.
What if they say "apply online"?
Thank them, ask if you can mention you called in your cover letter, and then apply. In Singapore, email once a week is acceptable – avoid multiple phone calls.
