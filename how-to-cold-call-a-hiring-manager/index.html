What Is Cold Calling a Hiring Manager?

Cold calling a hiring manager means contacting the person responsible for hiring at a company directly – without a prior introduction – to inquire about job opportunities. This is typically done by phone, but can also include email or LinkedIn messages.

The goal is to stand out from the crowd of online applicants, build a personal connection, and potentially uncover roles that haven't been advertised yet. When done professionally, cold calling can be one of the most effective ways to get interviews — and for job seekers targeting visa-sponsored roles specifically, it does something an online application can't: it lets you confirm, in real time, whether a company is genuinely open to sponsoring a work visa before you invest hours polishing an application for a role that was never going anywhere.

Career advisors have repeated the claim that "70–80% of jobs are never advertised" for decades. The exact figure is genuinely disputed — some researchers trace it to informal estimates rather than rigorous data — but the underlying pattern behind it holds up: companies consistently prefer referrals, direct approaches, and known quantities over sorting through an inbox of anonymous applications. You don't need the statistic to be exactly right to know that being a real person on the phone beats being one more line in an applicant-tracking system.

Why Cold Calling Hiring Managers Works

  • Bypasses Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) – A large share of online applications are filtered out by keyword-matching software before a human ever opens them. A call goes directly to the decision‑maker, no algorithm involved.
  • Demonstrates initiative – Employers consistently rank proactive candidates higher, especially for roles where independence and confidence matter — which is most roles.
  • Reveals unadvertised openings – Positions are frequently earmarked for an internal move or a known contact before a job ad is ever written. A call can put you in that conversation instead of the public queue.
  • Creates a memorable first impression – A confident, professional call can set you apart from dozens of near-identical CVs sitting in the same inbox.
  • Accelerates the process – You get immediate feedback instead of waiting days or weeks for an automated "we'll be in touch," and can sometimes move straight to scheduling an interview.
  • Lets you qualify the opportunity, not just sell yourself – A two-minute call can tell you whether a role is a real fit (timeline, visa policy, team size) before you spend an evening tailoring a CV to it.

Is it effective? Yes – when done correctly. A well‑prepared call can dramatically increase your chances of getting an interview compared to applying online alone, precisely because so few other candidates are willing to do it. Most of your competition won't pick up the phone. That's the opportunity.

The IFMOSA 5‑Step Method for Cold Calling Hiring Managers

This is our proprietary framework, developed from real‑world experience helping international job seekers land interviews with visa-sponsoring employers. Follow these steps to maximise your success rate.

Step 1: Identify the Company and the Role

Choose companies where you genuinely want to work. Research their industry, recent news, and the type of positions they typically hire for. Be specific – know which department or role you're targeting.

For visa-sponsorship candidates: prioritize companies with a documented history of sponsoring international hires. A company that has never sponsored a visa before is not impossible, but it means educating them on the process from zero — a much longer sales cycle than approaching a company that already has a sponsorship pathway in place.

Step 2: Identify the Decision‑Maker

Find the name and title of the person who would be your direct manager or the HR manager responsible for that department. Use LinkedIn, the company website, or call the main switchboard to ask.

Tip: Look for titles like "Head of [Department]", "[Department] Manager", "Director of [Department]", or "Recruiting Manager".

Step 3: Find Their Direct Contact Information

Once you have a name, find their direct phone number or email. Use:

  • LinkedIn – many profiles include contact details.
  • Company website – sometimes direct numbers are listed.
  • Email pattern – firstname.lastname@company.com (common format).
  • Phone directories – tools like Hunter.io or ZoomInfo can help.

Pro tip: If you can't find a direct number, call the main company line and ask to be connected to the person by name.

This is the step that eats the most time. Finding a verified direct WhatsApp number or email for the right person — and confirming the company actually sponsors visas before you call — can easily take an hour or more per company when you're doing it manually. Step 4: Make the Call – Use a Script, But Be Natural

Prepare a script that covers your introduction, purpose, and a few key questions. Practice it, but don't read it verbatim – sound conversational. Here's a template we recommend:

"Hello, my name is [Your Name]. I'm calling because I'm very interested in working with [Company Name] in [Department/Role]. I've researched your team and I believe my skills in [Key Skill] could be a great fit. Do you have a couple of minutes to chat about any upcoming opportunities?"

Adapt this to your style. Be concise, respectful, and have a clear ask. Always ask if it's a good time to talk.

Step 5: Follow Up Professionally

If the hiring manager is unavailable or asks you to send your CV, follow up promptly. Send a thank‑you email after the call, reiterating your interest and highlighting key points from the conversation.

If they don't answer, leave a brief, professional voicemail and try again a couple of days later. Persistence shows dedication – but avoid being pushy. Two to three attempts over a week or two is the right range; beyond that, shift to email so you're not seen as a nuisance.

Remember: The IFMOSA method isn't about being aggressive – it's about being prepared, professional, and persistent. When you follow these steps, you'll stand out as a serious candidate.

What to Say When You Call a Hiring Manager

Here are scenario‑based scripts you can adapt, including one written specifically for candidates who need visa sponsorship:

Scenario 1: You Know They Have an Open Position

"Hi [Hiring Manager's Name], this is [Your Name]. I saw your job posting for [Role] on [Platform]. I'm very interested, and I wanted to introduce myself directly and ask if you have a moment to discuss what you're looking for in the ideal candidate."

Scenario 2: You're Exploring Unadvertised Opportunities

"Hello [Hiring Manager's Name], this is [Your Name]. I've been following [Company Name] for a while and I'm impressed by [specific project/news]. I'm a [Your Profession] with [X years] experience in [area]. I was wondering if you have any upcoming needs in your team, even if they're not yet advertised."

Scenario 3: You've Already Applied Online

"Good morning [Hiring Manager's Name], this is [Your Name]. I recently applied for the [Role] position via your careers portal. I wanted to call and briefly introduce myself to put a voice to the application, and ask if you have any questions about my background."

Scenario 4: You Need Visa Sponsorship

"Hi [Hiring Manager's Name], this is [Your Name]. I'm reaching out about [Role/Department] at [Company Name]. I have [X years] of experience in [area], and I'm currently based in [Country], which means the role would involve visa sponsorship. Before I take up more of your time — is that something your team is able to support for the right candidate?"

Notice this script leads with experience and value, and treats the visa question as a quick qualifying check rather than an apology. Asking directly and early — after establishing genuine interest — respects everyone's time and avoids a wasted process on either side if sponsorship truly isn't possible.

Tip: Always end with a clear next step – whether it's sending your CV, scheduling a meeting, or asking when to follow up.

Cold Calling vs. Cold Emailing a Hiring Manager

Neither approach beats the other outright — the smartest job seekers use both, in sequence. Here's how they compare:

📞 Cold Calling

  • Real-time answers — you know within minutes if there's interest
  • Much harder to ignore than an email sitting in a crowded inbox
  • Shows confidence and communication skills directly
  • Higher pressure — requires preparation and comfort thinking on your feet
  • Doesn't scale — you can realistically make a handful of quality calls per day

✉️ Cold Emailing

  • Low-pressure — you can write, edit, and send on your own time
  • Scales further — you can reach more companies per day
  • Easy for the recipient to skim, deprioritize, or forget entirely
  • Gives the hiring manager something to forward internally if they're interested
  • Useful as a warm-up before calling, or a follow-up after a call goes to voicemail

A strong pattern: send a short, specific email first thing in the morning, then follow up with a call that same afternoon referencing it. You'll sound prepared, not random — and if they haven't opened the email yet, the call itself becomes the memorable first touch.

Best Time to Call a Hiring Manager

  • Day of the week: Tuesdays, Wednesdays, and Thursdays are optimal. Avoid Mondays (busy) and Fridays (people winding down).
  • Time of day: 10:00 – 11:30 AM and 2:00 – 4:00 PM are ideal. Avoid lunch hours (12:00 – 1:30 PM) and the first/last hour of the day.
  • Seasonal: Avoid major holiday periods and the first week of the new year when many are on leave.
  • Time zones: If you're calling internationally, double-check the destination's local time and business hours — a call that lands at their 7 AM or 8 PM, however well-intentioned, undermines the professional impression you're going for.

Before calling, have ready:

  • Your CV, tailored (or at least tailorable) to the role
  • A notepad to jot names, questions, and next steps during the call
  • Two or three specific questions about the company or role
  • A one-line answer prepared for "why are you interested in relocating / working here specifically"

The more prepared you are, the more confident and professional you'll sound — and confidence is most of what a hiring manager is actually evaluating in a two-minute unplanned call.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Cold Calling

Reading your script word-for-word. It's obvious to the person on the other end, and it kills the natural, confident tone that makes cold calls work in the first place. Know your key points; don't recite them.

Calling with no idea who you're calling. Dialing the general switchboard and asking "do you have any jobs?" wastes everyone's time. Always aim for a specific name and role.

Opening with your visa situation before establishing any interest. Leading with "I need sponsorship" before the hiring manager knows anything about you invites an instant no. Lead with value; qualify sponsorship once there's real interest.

Calling every company on your list back-to-back with no notes. You will lose track of who said what. Log every call — name, date, outcome, and next step — so your follow-ups are specific, not generic.

Treating a "no" as final. Hiring needs change. A "not right now" from three months ago is worth a polite follow-up today, especially if you've gained new experience since.

Giving up after one unanswered call. Voicemail-and-move-on is normal, not a rejection. Two to three attempts over a week or two is standard professional persistence, not pestering.

How to Handle Common Objections

"Send me your CV and we'll review it."

Response: "Absolutely, I'll send it right after this call. Just so I can tailor it to your needs, could you tell me a bit more about the role or what you're looking for?"

"We're not hiring right now."

Response: "I understand. Would it be okay if I check back with you in a few months? I'm really interested in your company, and I'd love to stay on your radar."

"I'm busy – can you call back later?"

Response: "Of course. When would be a better time to reach you? I'll make sure to call then."

"We don't sponsor visas."

Response: "That's completely understandable, thank you for letting me know directly — I appreciate you being upfront. Is that a fixed company policy, or could it vary case by case for the right candidate?" If the answer is a firm no, thank them and move on immediately. Confirming this early is a win, not a loss — it saves you from investing further time in a dead end.

"Why didn't you just apply online like everyone else?"

Response: "I did want to make sure my application actually reached the right person, and I was genuinely curious to learn more about the role directly from you before submitting anything." This reframes the call as diligence, not desperation.

Always be polite and respectful. The goal is to open a dialogue, not to pressure.

Cold Calling for Visa-Sponsored Roles: What's Different

Cold calling works the same way whether or not a visa is involved — but for international candidates, there are a few extra layers worth planning for before you dial:

  • Verify sponsorship history before you call, not during. Calling a company that has never sponsored a visa and asking them to consider it live, mid-conversation, is a much harder sell than approaching one with an established pathway.
  • Know your own visa category and timeline. Being able to answer "how soon could you start" and "what type of visa would this require" confidently signals that you've done your homework — and that you're not asking the employer to figure out immigration law on your behalf.
  • Lead with the business case, not the personal one. "I bring [specific skill/experience] that's hard to find locally" lands better than "I really want to move to your country." Employers sponsor visas to solve a hiring problem, not as a favor.
  • Be ready for a slower yes. Even an enthusiastic hiring manager may need to loop in HR, legal, or leadership before committing to sponsorship. A positive call is a strong first step, not a guaranteed offer — follow up accordingly.

Frequently Asked Questions About Cold Calling Hiring Managers

Can I call a hiring manager directly?
Yes, you can – and it's often more effective than applying online. Just be polite, professional, and prepared.
What should I say when I call a hiring manager?
Introduce yourself, state your interest in the company/department, and ask if they have a few minutes to chat. Use the scripts provided above as a starting point.
Is it better to call before or after applying online?
Both can work. Calling before helps you tailor your application; calling after shows persistence. Choose the approach that feels most natural.
What if I don't know the hiring manager's name?
Do your research – LinkedIn, company website, or calling the main switchboard to ask. If you can't find it, address the call to "the hiring manager" or "the department head."
What time is best to call a hiring manager?
Mid‑morning (10‑11:30 AM) or mid‑afternoon (2‑4 PM) on Tuesday‑Thursday are ideal. Avoid Monday mornings and Friday afternoons.
Should I leave a voicemail if they don't answer?
Yes – leave a brief, professional voicemail with your name, number, and a concise reason for calling. Then follow up later.
How many times should I call if they don't answer?
Try two to three times over a week or two, then switch to email. Don't be a nuisance – be persistent but respectful.
Does cold calling work for jobs that require visa sponsorship?
Yes, but framing matters. Lead with your qualifications and interest first, and confirm sponsorship once there's genuine mutual interest rather than opening with it. Targeting companies with a track record of sponsoring visas makes the biggest difference.
How is cold calling different from cold emailing a hiring manager?
A call gets real-time feedback and is harder to ignore than an email in a crowded inbox, but requires you to think on your feet. Email is lower-pressure and scales further. Many successful job seekers combine both.
What if the hiring manager says they don't sponsor visas?
Ask politely whether that's a fixed policy or specific to this role, thank them for their time, and move on. Confirming this early saves you from chasing a dead-end application later.
